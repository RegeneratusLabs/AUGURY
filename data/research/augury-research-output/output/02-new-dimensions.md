# Track 2: New Indicator Dimensions for AUGURY

> **Research output**: Discovered indicator dimensions beyond the current 5 (moisture, pH, fertility, structure, salinity).
> **Date**: July 2026
> **Status**: Draft — candidate dimensions for database expansion

---

## Overview

Research across 12 candidate dimensions yielded strong evidence for 11 viable new indicator dimensions. One dimension (microbiome state) has more indirect than direct weed-to-microbiome mapping and is flagged for further validation. Each dimension includes at least 3 indicator species with documented evidence.

**Summary of findings:**

| # | Dimension | Viable? | Species found | Key insight |
|---|-----------|---------|---------------|-------------|
| 1 | Soil Biology (Mycorrhizae) | ✅ Yes | 8+ | Non-mycorrhizal weed families = disturbed soil signal |
| 2 | Compaction Depth | ✅ Yes | 7 | Different root architectures indicate different compaction depths |
| 3 | Water Table Depth | ✅ Yes | 8 | Phreatophyte weeds directly map to groundwater depth ranges |
| 4 | Aeration / Redox | ✅ Yes | 6 | Anaerobic indicator species well documented |
| 5 | Organic Matter | ✅ Yes | 10 | Strong OM indicators identified via Ducerf's encyclopedia |

| 8 | Microbiome State (F:B) | ⚠️ Indirect | 4 | Proxy indicators through mycorrhizal status; needs direct validation |

## Dimension 1: Soil Biology (Mycorrhizal Associations)

**Summary:** Certain plant families are constitutively non-mycorrhizal — their presence in weed communities signals disrupted fungal networks and bacterially-dominated soil. This dimension allows AUGURY to infer below-ground microbiome disruption from above-ground weed composition alone.

### Species: Brassicaceae family (mustards, shepherd's purse, wild radish — *Capsella bursa-pastoris*, *Sinapis arvensis*, *Raphanus raphanistrum*, *Brassica* spp.)
**Indicator value:** Non-mycorrhizal soil; disrupted arbuscular mycorrhizal fungal (AMF) networks; bacterial-dominant conditions
**Source:** Cosme et al. (2018), "Non-Mycorrhizal Plants: The Exceptions that Prove the Rule," *Trends in Plant Science*; Brundrett (1991), "Mycorrhizas in Natural Ecosystems," *Advances in Ecological Research*; PNW Handbooks, "Nonmycorrhizal Plant Families"
**Confidence:** High — Brassicaceae non-mycorrhizal status is universally accepted in mycorrhizal literature
**Practical value to AUGURY:** When Brassicaceae weeds dominate, infer low AMF colonization potential for subsequent crops. Recommend mycorrhizal inoculants or cover crops from mycorrhizal families.

### Species: Chenopodiaceae/Amaranthaceae family (*Chenopodium album* — fat hen/lambsquarters, *Amaranthus retroflexus* — redroot pigweed, *Kochia scoparia* — summer cypress)
**Indicator value:** Non-mycorrhizal; high bacterial activity; often associated with excess uncomposted animal organic matter (manure applications)
**Source:** PNW Pest Management Handbooks; Ducerf, G. (2014), *L'encyclopédie des plantes bio-indicatrices*, Vol. 1, Promonature; Maughan & Amos (2021), "Weeds as Bioindicators: A Farmer's Field Guide," CAWR
**Confidence:** High — multiple sources corroborate non-mycorrhizal status and bacterial association
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Chenopodium + Amaranthus communities signal bacterial-dominant soil with disrupted fungal hyphal networks. Flag for reduced tillage and fungal compost amendments.

### Species: Polygonaceae family (*Polygonum aviculare* — prostrate knotweed, *Rumex acetosella* — sheep's sorrel, *Fallopia convolvulus* — wild buckwheat)
**Indicator value:** Non-mycorrhizal or weakly mycorrhizal; disturbed, often compacted soil with low fungal biomass
**Source:** Brundrett (1991); mycorrhizas.info "Nonmycorrhizal Plants" database; Ducerf (2014)
**Confidence:** Medium-high — Polygonaceae is listed as non-mycorrhizal with exceptions noted in some genera
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Combined with compaction indicators, these species signal multi-factor soil degradation including fungal network loss.

### Species: *Stellaria media* (common chickweed — Caryophyllaceae)
**Indicator value:** Non-mycorrhizal family member; thrives in high-fertility, bacterially-processed nitrogen
**Source:** PNW Handbooks; Almanac.com "What Weeds Say About Your Soil"
**Confidence:** Medium — Caryophyllaceae listed as non-mycorrhizal but chickweed often grows in healthy garden soils too
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Use in combination with other non-mycorrhizal species for stronger inference

### Additional notes — Mycorrhizal weed species:
- *Plantago lanceolata* (ribwort plantain) — strongly mycorrhizal; indicates potential for AMF recovery
- *Cichorium intybus* (chicory) — mycorrhizal; deep taproot extends fungal pathways
- *Taraxacum officinale* (dandelion) — mycorrhizal
- *Trifolium repens* (white clover) — mycorrhizal
- Mycorrhizal weed presence in a community dominated by non-mycorrhizal species can indicate transition zones or recovery trajectories

## Dimension 2: Compaction Depth

**Summary:** Weed root architecture maps to compaction depth. Surface-creeping and fibrous-rooted weeds indicate shallow/surface compaction; deep taprooted weeds indicate compaction at tillage pan or subsoil depth. This dimension adds depth-specificity beyond the current binary "structure" dimension.

### Species: *Polygonum aviculare* (prostrate knotweed), *Poa annua* (annual meadowgrass), *Stellaria media* (chickweed)
**Indicator value:** Surface compaction / crusted surface / hardpan within top 5-10 cm. These species have prostrate growth, fibrous shallow roots, and adapt to trampled soil
**Source:** Maughan & Amos (2021), Bioindicators Field Guide (CAWR); Fairfax Gardening "Indicator Weeds Table"; Almanac.com
**Confidence:** High — multiple sources consistently list these as surface compaction indicators (Poa annua: 3 sources; Polygonum aviculare: 4 sources; Stellaria media: 4 sources)
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Surface compactor community → recommend mechanical aeration of top 5-10 cm; no deep ripping needed

### Species: *Plantago major* (broadleaf plantain), *Plantago lanceolata* (ribwort plantain)
**Indicator value:** Moderate compaction, often in clay soils; survives trampling; indicates compaction in upper 15-20 cm
**Source:** Maughan & Amos (2021) — 3 sources corroborated; Almanac.com; MSU Extension; UConn IPM
**Confidence:** High — one of the most universally recognized compaction indicators
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Plantain-dominated communities → moderate compaction; core aeration + organic matter amendment recommended

### Species: *Taraxacum officinale* (dandelion), *Cichorium intybus* (chicory), *Arctium lappa* (burdock)
**Indicator value:** Deep compaction / subsoil compaction / tillage pan below 15-20 cm. These species develop strong taproots that "bio-drill" through compacted layers
**Source:** Almanac.com; MSU Extension; WSU Extension FS396E "Soil Compaction in Annual Crop Production"; Maughan & Amos (2021) — Taraxacum: 3 sources; Cichorium: noted as compaction indicator in base-rich soils provoking anaerobic conditions
**Confidence:** High — taproot-compaction relationship well documented in both agricultural extension and bioindicator literature
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Taproot weed community → deep compaction or plough pan; recommend deep ripping or biodrilling cover crops (tillage radish, fava bean)

### Species: *Elytrigia repens* (couch grass / quackgrass), *Cirsium arvense* (creeping thistle)
**Indicator value:** Smear layer / plough pan compaction; these rhizomatous perennials proliferate at the depth of a compacted layer where their rhizomes can spread horizontally
**Source:** Maughan & Amos (2021) — Elytrigia noted for "smear layer / compaction of loamy soils with high pH / over tillage"; Cirsium noted for "smear layer"
**Confidence:** Medium — documented in Ducerf's encyclopedia but not widely corroborated in English-language extension literature
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Rhizomatous perennial community with these species → compacted pan at tillage depth; structural intervention needed

### Species: *Convolvulus arvensis* (field bindweed), *Ranunculus repens* (creeping buttercup)
**Indicator value:** Crusted or compacted surface; bindweed indicates "hardpan or crusty surface" (3-4 corroborating sources); buttercup indicates compaction of wet-cultivated soil
**Source:** Maughan & Amos (2021); Almanac.com; Fairfax Gardening table
**Confidence:** Medium-high — multiple sources corroborate
**Practical value to AUGURY:** These species in combination with surface compactors strengthen the depth-specific diagnosis

## Dimension 3: Water Table Depth

**Summary:** Phreatophyte (groundwater-dependent) plant species are well-established indicators of water table depth. Obligate phreatophytes indicate accessible groundwater within root reach; their presence in non-riparian settings flags shallow water tables. This dimension distinguishes between surface waterlogging (in current "moisture" dimension) and deep groundwater accessibility.

### Species: *Equisetum arvense* (field horsetail)
**Indicator value:** Shallow water table; species roots can reach down to water-saturated layers; Ducerf specifically notes "water table" as an indication
**Source:** Maughan & Amos (2021); MSU Extension (Sprague, C. — "poorly drained soils"); Meinzer, O.E. (1927), "Plants as Indicators of Ground Water," USGS Water Supply Paper 577
**Confidence:** Medium — water table association noted by Ducerf; broader literature frames it as wet/poorly drained soil indicator
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Distinguished from surface waterlogging species: horsetail indicates water table within ~2m; drainage likely ineffective — groundwater management needed

### Species: *Juncus* spp. (rushes), *Carex* spp. (sedges), *Phragmites australis* (common reed)
**Indicator value:** High water table / shallow groundwater; these wetland genera are classic indicators of water within 0-1.5m of surface
**Source:** Meinzer (1927); USGS "Plants as Indicators of Ground Water"; Maughan & Amos (2021) — Juncus and Carex both noted for "wet/waterlogged/poorly drained"
**Confidence:** High — universally recognized wetland/high-water-table indicators
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Distinguish from seasonal surface ponding — perennial Juncus/Carex indicates persistent shallow groundwater

### Species: *Tamarix* spp. (saltcedar / tamarisk)
**Indicator value:** Deep water table accessible (roots reach 30m+ depth); facultative phreatophyte; also indicates increased surface salinity. Can lower water table through high transpiration (up to 200 gallons/day per plant)
**Source:** USFWS Ecological Risk Screening Summary for Tamarix; USDA FEIS database; NASA SVS "Invasive Species: Tamarisk's Use of Water"; Meinzer (1927)
**Confidence:** High — one of the most studied phreatophytic invasive species
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Presence indicates accessible groundwater at depth (5-30m) in arid/semi-arid regions; also flags salinization risk

### Species: *Prosopis* spp. (mesquite — *P. glandulosa*, *P. velutina*, *P. pubescens*)
**Indicator value:** Obligate phreatophyte; indicates accessible groundwater, typically at depths of 3-15m. Roots documented to 53m depth
**Source:** Meinzer (1927); USFWS Tamarix Ecological Risk Screening (notes Prosopis as obligate phreatophyte affected by water table lowering); USGS publications
**Confidence:** High — classic phreatophyte in USGS literature
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Presence in non-riparian arid setting = accessible deep groundwater; key indicator for dryland water resource assessment

### Species: *Sarcobatus vermiculatus* (black greasewood), *Atriplex* spp. (saltbushes — *A. canescens*, *A. confertifolia*)
**Indicator value:** Phreatophytic or facultative phreatophyte; indicates moderately deep water table (2-10m) in alkaline/saline basins
**Source:** Meinzer (1927); USGS "Plants as Indicators of Ground Water"; USDA FEIS Tamarix documentation
**Confidence:** Medium-high — well documented in USGS phreatophyte classic literature
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Presence indicates both accessible groundwater AND elevated salinity (distinguishes from non-saline groundwater indicators)

### Species: *Populus* spp. (cottonwoods), *Salix* spp. (willows)
**Indicator value:** Obligate phreatophytes; indicate water table within 1-5m in riparian corridors
**Source:** Meinzer (1927); Naumburg et al. (2005), "Phreatophytic vegetation and groundwater fluctuations," *Environmental Management*
**Confidence:** High — classical phreatophyte indicators
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Although typically not "weeds," volunteer cottonwood/willow in agricultural settings flags shallow water table

## Dimension 4: Soil Aeration / Redox Status

**Summary:** Several weed species are reliable indicators of anaerobic soil conditions, while others indicate well-aerated soils. This dimension captures the oxygen status of the soil profile — a critical parameter for root health, nutrient availability, and microbial activity that goes beyond simple "moisture" or "drainage."

### Species: *Equisetum arvense* (field horsetail)
**Indicator value:** Anaerobic soil conditions; poorly aerated; waterlogged. Also indicates low pH as a co-factor
**Source:** MSU Extension (Sprague, C.); Maughan & Amos (2021) — noted for "humid soil / smear layer / water table"; multiple corroborating sources
**Confidence:** High — consistently reported across extension and bioindicator literature
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Combined anaerobic indicator; presence flags both poor aeration AND low pH — a compounding stress for most crops

### Species: *Ranunculus repens* (creeping buttercup)
**Indicator value:** Anaerobic conditions from wet-compacted soil ("cultivation of the soil when wet"); hydric soils
**Source:** Maughan & Amos (2021) — 3 corroborating sources; Fairfax Gardening table
**Confidence:** High — buttercup's preference for wet anaerobic soil is widely recognized
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Presence suggests soil was worked when too wet, creating anaerobic smear layer; avoid cultivation when wet

### Species: *Sonchus oleraceus* (common sow-thistle), *Sonchus asper* (prickly sow-thistle)
**Indicator value:** Waterlogging / anaerobic conditions explicitly noted as indicator; excess carbon leading to anaerobic microbial metabolism
**Source:** Maughan & Amos (2021) — Sonchus oleraceus: 2 sources; Sonchus asper: 2 sources, specifically noting "waterlogging / anaerobic"
**Confidence:** Medium — documented in Ducerf but less in English extension literature
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Sow-thistle + high organic matter → anaerobic decomposition; improve drainage or reduce organic inputs

### Species: *Plantago major* (broadleaf plantain) in wet conditions
**Indicator value:** Anaerobiosis / hydric soils when combined with waterlogging signs
**Source:** Maughan & Amos (2021) — Plantago spp. noted for "anaerobiosis / hydric soils"; UConn IPM
**Confidence:** Medium — plantain is a generalist but develops anaerobic soil indicator value when in wet areas
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Stunted, wet-area plantain indicates combined compaction + anaerobic stress

### Species: *Persicaria maculosa* (redshank / lady's thumb) — CONTRAST INDICATOR
**Indicator value:** Well-aerated soil (noted as "well aerated" in bioindicator guide); when this species is absent from otherwise wet areas, it confirms anaerobic conditions in those areas
**Source:** Maughan & Amos (2021) — multiple corroborating sources for "well aerated"
**Confidence:** Medium — well-documented positive indicator of aeration
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Presence = good aeration; absence from wet patches = anaerobic confirmation

### Species: *Viola arvensis* (field pansy) — CONTRAST INDICATOR
**Indicator value:** Aerated, well-structured soil; specifically noted as "aerated" and "watered but not waterlogged"
**Source:** Maughan & Amos (2021); Ducerf (2014, 2013)
**Confidence:** Medium — documented in Ducerf's encyclopedia
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Field pansy presence indicates good soil aeration + low humus (combined indicator)

## Dimension 5: Organic Matter (OM) Status

**Summary:** Weed communities provide strong signals about soil organic matter levels — from humus-rich fertile soils to depleted, low-OM conditions. This dimension adds specificity beyond the general "fertility" category by distinguishing mineral fertility from organic matter/humus status.

### Species indicating HIGH organic matter / humus:

### Species: *Chenopodium album* (fat hen / lambsquarters)
**Indicator value:** High fertility/humus; excess uncomposted animal organic matter; rich in nitrogen at surface
**Source:** Maughan & Amos (2021) — 5 corroborating sources (maximum confidence); Almanac.com ("rich soil, high nitrogen"); MSU Extension
**Confidence:** Very High — the single most consistently cited high-fertility weed indicator across all sources
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Dominant Chenopodium → high OM with rapid N mineralization; manage for N loss risk

### Species: *Stellaria media* (common chickweed)
**Indicator value:** High fertility/humus; "soil balance"; surplus nitrogen at surface; nutrient-rich soils
**Source:** Maughan & Amos (2021) — 3-4 corroborating sources; Almanac.com ("high nitrogen, rich soil"); UConn IPM
**Confidence:** High — universally recognized high-fertility indicator
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Chickweed + fat hen community → very high OM and N; ideal for heavy-feeder crops

### Species: *Urtica dioica* (stinging nettle)
**Indicator value:** Nutrient-rich soil; excess carbon; acid or low-lime; change of state of iron in soil
**Source:** Maughan & Amos (2021) — 3 sources; Almanac.com ("rich, acidic soil"); MSU Extension
**Confidence:** High — classic indicator of fertile, humus-rich, often slightly acidic soils
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Nettle patches map ancient settlement/midden sites in Europe; indicates long-term OM accumulation

### Species: *Senecio vulgaris* (common groundsel)
**Indicator value:** High fertility/humus; excess carbon; base-rich soils, pH >6
**Source:** Maughan & Amos (2021); Almanac.com ("fertile, nutrient-rich soil," "If you have groundsel, you have good soil!")
**Confidence:** High — well-recognized fertile soil indicator
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Quick annual indicator of high-fertility conditions

### Species: *Holcus lanatus* (Yorkshire fog), *Lamium amplexicaule* (henbit)
**Indicator value:** Holcus: high fertility, rich in OM; Lamium: nutrient rich, excess carbon, erosion/leaching in base-rich soils
**Source:** Maughan & Amos (2021); Almanac.com (henbit: "high nitrogen")
**Confidence:** Medium-high — multiple sources corroborate
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Grassland/pasture OM indicators

### Species indicating LOW organic matter / humus deficiency:

### Species: *Spergula arvensis* (corn spurry)
**Indicator value:** Low humus / calcium deficiency / "loss of CHC binding power due to deficiency in stable humus"; leaching and erosion due to lack of soil cover; acid or low lime sandy soils
**Source:** Maughan & Amos (2021) — 3-4 corroborating sources; Ducerf (2014)
**Confidence:** High — consistently documented in Ducerf's encyclopedia as a low-humus specialist
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Corn spurry community → critically low OM; urgent organic amendment needed; calcium deficiency likely co-occurs

### Species: *Viola arvensis* (field pansy)
**Indicator value:** "Lack of humus"; acid or low lime, sandy/light soils; low nitrogen; aerated
**Source:** Maughan & Amos (2021); Ducerf (2013, Vol. 3)
**Confidence:** Medium — documented in Ducerf; less corroboration from other sources
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Field pansy + corn spurry = strong low-OM signal in sandy acid soils

### Species: *Rumex acetosella* (sheep's sorrel)
**Indicator value:** Low fertility; "absence, loss of humus due to lack of manure (N and P input)"; acid sandy soils
**Source:** Maughan & Amos (2021); Almanac.com ("dry, sandy, acidic soil depleted of nutrients and low in calcium")
**Confidence:** Medium-high — multiple sources corroborate low-fertility association
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Sheep sorrel indicates depleted, acid, sandy soil needing comprehensive amendment

### Species: *Centaurea maculosa* (spotted knapweed), *Achillea millefolium* (yarrow)
**Indicator value:** Knapweed: low calcium, low humus, very low phosphorus; Yarrow: loss of OM, erosion from tillage/overgrazing, low potassium
**Source:** MSU Extension; Maughan & Amos (2021)
**Confidence:** Medium — documented but primarily from single primary source (MSU/Ducerf)
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Knapweed + yarrow = degraded rangeland/pasture with OM loss and mineral imbalance

## Dimension 6: Mineral Balance (Ca, Mg, K, P)

**Summary:** Individual weed species show strong correlations with specific mineral excesses and deficiencies — notably calcium, magnesium, potassium, and phosphorus. This dimension provides species-level mineral diagnosis beyond the current general "fertility" dimension.

### Calcium Deficiency Indicators:

### Species: *Taraxacum officinale* (dandelion)
**Indicator value:** Low calcium; also indicates compacted soil and high potassium
**Source:** MSU Extension; Almanac.com ("compacted soil, low calcium"); eatmyshrubs.com ("low calcium, high magnesium")
**Confidence:** High — broadly recognized calcium deficiency indicator
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Dandelion dominance → soil test for Ca:Mg ratio; likely needs liming (if acid) or gypsum (if pH-neutral)

### Species: *Rumex crispus* (curly dock)
**Indicator value:** Low calcium, extremely high magnesium, high phosphorus, high potassium; also compacted soil
**Source:** MSU Extension
**Confidence:** Medium — well-documented at MSU but from single primary source
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Curly dock is a mineral imbalance specialist — presence flags severe cation imbalance needing soil test

### Species: *Oxalis* spp. (wood sorrel)
**Indicator value:** Low calcium, high magnesium
**Source:** Almanac.com; eatmyshrubs.com
**Confidence:** Medium — recognized in gardening literature but lacks formal research backing
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Oxalis presence → test Ca:Mg ratio; magnesium likely in excess relative to calcium

### Species: *Equisetum arvense* (field horsetail), *Urtica dioica* (stinging nettle), *Helenium tenuifolium* (bitterweed), *Andropogon virginicus* (broomsedge), *Fallopia convolvulus* (wild buckwheat)
**Indicator value:** All indicate calcium deficiency per MSU Extension
**Source:** MSU Extension (Sprague, C.)
**Confidence:** Medium — documented at MSU; species-level validation varies
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Community-level Ca deficiency signal when multiple of these species co-occur

### Magnesium Excess Indicators:

### Species: *Rumex crispus* (curly dock), *Oxalis* spp. (wood sorrel), *Hordeum jubatum* (foxtail barley), *Chrysanthemum leucanthemum* (oxeye daisy)
**Indicator value:** High magnesium relative to calcium; Mg-dominated cation exchange
**Source:** MSU Extension; Almanac.com
**Confidence:** Medium — consistent pattern across extension sources
**Practical value to AUGURY:** High-Mg indicator community → gypsum application (Ca without pH change) to rebalance cations

### Potassium Excess/Phosphorus Deficiency Indicators:

### Species: *Chenopodium album* (lambsquarters)
**Indicator value:** Low phosphorus, high potassium (per MSU); also noted as "low P? high K?" with uncertainty in bioindicator guide
**Source:** MSU Extension; Maughan & Amos (2021)
**Confidence:** Medium — sources agree on K excess but disagree slightly on P status
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Lambsquarters + high K signal → reduce K inputs; may indicate manure-heavy fertilization

### Species: *Amaranthus retroflexus* (redroot pigweed)
**Indicator value:** High potassium and manganese; low phosphorus and calcium; iron-manganese ratio out of balance
**Source:** MSU Extension
**Confidence:** Medium — documented at MSU
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Pigweed community → test for micronutrient imbalance (Fe:Mn ratio) alongside Ca deficiency

### Species: *Arctium lappa* (burdock), *Centaurea maculosa* (knapweed), *Chrysanthemum leucanthemum* (oxeye daisy)
**Indicator value:** All indicate low phosphorus with elevated potassium/magnesium per MSU
**Source:** MSU Extension; Almanac.com
**Confidence:** Medium
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Multiple aster-family weeds with this mineral signature = strong P deficiency signal

### Species: *Portulaca oleracea* (purslane)
**Indicator value:** High phosphorus indicator (contrast indicator); prefers rich soil with high available P
**Source:** Almanac.com
**Confidence:** Low-Medium — primarily from popular gardening literature
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Purslane as positive P indicator (rather than deficiency) — useful contrast species

### Boron / Micronutrient Indicators (limited data):

### Species: *Spergula arvensis* (corn spurry)
**Indicator value:** Calcium deficiency and potential boron deficiency co-indicator (sandy acid soils prone to boron leaching)
**Source:** Inferred from soil chemistry of preferred habitat (Ducerf 2014; sandy acid low-humus soils)
**Confidence:** Low — boron association is indirect inference, not direct documentation
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Corn spurry in sandy soils → test for boron alongside Ca and OM

> **Note on boron, zinc, manganese indicators:** Direct species-level documentation for micronutrient deficiencies (B, Zn, Mn, Cu, Fe) is sparse in the weed bioindicator literature. The MSU Extension reference mentions Mn (iron-manganese ratio via pigweed) but most micronutrient weed indicators remain undocumented. This is a research gap for future investigation. AUGURY could pioneer this by correlating weed survey data with soil micronutrient tests.

## Dimension 7: Soil Type / Texture

**Summary:** Weed species show clear preferences for clay/heavy vs sandy/light vs loam soils. This dimension provides texture inference from weed community composition — valuable for regions without detailed soil maps.

### Clay / Heavy Soil Indicators:

### Species: *Taraxacum officinale* (dandelion)
**Indicator value:** Clay/heavy soils (3 corroborating sources); also noted for acid or low lime soils
**Source:** Maughan & Amos (2021); Almanac.com; multiple gardening sources
**Confidence:** High — widely recognized clay soil indicator
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Dandelion + other clay indicators → heavy clay soil diagnosis

### Species: *Plantago* spp. (plantains — *P. major*, *P. lanceolata*, *P. media*)
**Indicator value:** Clay/heavy soils (3 corroborating sources)
**Source:** Maughan & Amos (2021); Almanac.com ("compacted, clay-heavy, low fertility soil")
**Confidence:** High — consistently reported
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Plantain species are classic clay indicators, especially combined with compaction

### Species: *Ranunculus repens* (creeping buttercup)
**Indicator value:** Clay/heavy soils; lime deficiency; compacted, wet-cultivated soil
**Source:** Maughan & Amos (2021) — 3 corroborating sources
**Confidence:** High — well-documented clay/wet soil indicator
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Buttercup signals clay + poor drainage + low calcium

### Species: *Cirsium arvense* (creeping thistle), *Cichorium intybus* (chicory), *Tussilago farfara* (colt's foot), *Sonchus oleraceus* (sow-thistle)
**Indicator value:** All documented as clay/heavy soil indicators
**Source:** Maughan & Amos (2021) — each with at least 1-2 sources
**Confidence:** Medium — multiple species-pairs corroborate the pattern
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Community of clay indicators strengthens texture inference

### Sandy / Light Soil Indicators:

### Species: *Spergula arvensis* (corn spurry)
**Indicator value:** Sand/light soils (3-4 corroborating sources); strongly associated with sandy acid soils
**Source:** Maughan & Amos (2021); Ducerf (2014)
**Confidence:** High — one of the most specific sandy soil indicators
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Corn spurry = sandy soil with very low nutrient/water retention capacity

### Species: *Viola arvensis* (field pansy)
**Indicator value:** Sand/light soils; acid or low lime; lack of humus; aerated
**Source:** Maughan & Amos (2021); Ducerf (2013)
**Confidence:** Medium — well-documented in Ducerf
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Field pansy + corn spurry = classic sandy acid soil community

### Species: *Rumex acetosella* (sheep's sorrel)
**Indicator value:** Sand/light soils (source disagreement — also noted for clay-limestone); acid or low lime; low fertility
**Source:** Maughan & Amos (2021) — source disagreement noted; Almanac.com ("dry, sandy, acidic soil")
**Confidence:** Medium — some disagreement on texture but strong agreement on acid, low-fertility conditions
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Sheep sorrel reliably indicates acid, depleted soil; texture inference less certain

### Species: *Equisetum arvense* (field horsetail), *Convolvulus arvensis* (field bindweed), *Urtica dioica* (stinging nettle)
**Indicator value:** All noted for sand/light soil preference (with caveats — bindweed also does well in deep loose loams)
**Source:** Maughan & Amos (2021)
**Confidence:** Low-Medium — species are flexible across textures
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Use as supporting indicators, not primary texture diagnostics

### Loam / Balanced Soil Indicators:

### Species: *Chenopodium album* (fat hen), *Stellaria media* (chickweed), *Veronica persica* (common field speedwell)
**Indicator value:** Loam / medium-textured soils; well-balanced, fertile conditions
**Source:** Maughan & Amos (2021); multiple gardening sources
**Confidence:** Medium — loam preference implied by high-fertility association
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Community dominated by these species → likely loam soil with good structure

### Scribd reference — additional texture indicators:
- Sandy soil: sandbur (*Cenchrus* spp.), dog fennel (*Eupatorium capillifolium*), cornflower (*Centaurea cyanus*)
- Heavy soil: wild garlic (*Allium vineale*), dandelion, broadleaf dock (*Rumex obtusifolius*)
- Source: "Weeds as Soil Condition Indicators" (Scribd, unattributed compilation)

## Dimension 8: Microbiome State (Fungal:Bacterial Ratio)

**Summary:** Direct weed-to-microbiome mapping is the least developed of all researched dimensions. However, non-mycorrhizal weed families serve as proxy indicators for bacterially-dominated, fungal-poor soils. This dimension is flagged for further research rather than immediate database integration.

**⚠️ Status: Indirect inference only — requires field validation before database integration**

### Species: Brassicaceae family weeds (aggregate)
**Indicator value:** Bacterial-dominant soil. Brassicaceae are non-mycorrhizal and their glucosinolate root exudates can suppress fungal pathogens. Their dominance signals fungal-poor, bacterial-rich conditions typical of frequently tilled agricultural soils
**Source:** Trautwig et al. (2023), "Reviewing ecological implications of mycorrhizal fungal..." *Frontiers in Plant Science*; Pagliarani et al. (2025), "The nonhost mycorrhizal status of weeds," *Frontiers in Agronomy*; Wang et al. (2021), "Non-host plants: Are they mycorrhizal networks players?" *PMC*
**Confidence:** Low-Medium — mechanism is well understood but direct "weed species → F:B ratio" calibration data is absent
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Recommend as a secondary inference layer: if non-mycorrhizal weed families dominate → flag "likely bacterial-dominant soil — confirm with soil biology test"

### Species: *Chenopodium album* (fat hen), *Amaranthus* spp. (pigweed)
**Indicator value:** Bacterial-dominant; Chenopodiaceae/Amaranthaceae are non-mycorrhizal; Chenopodium explicitly associated with "excess uncomposted animal OM" which drives bacterial blooms
**Source:** Ducerf (2014); Maughan & Amos (2021); PNW Handbooks
**Confidence:** Low-Medium — indirect through mycorrhizal status + OM association
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Same inference framework as Brassicaceae

### Species: Mycorrhizal perennial weeds — *Plantago lanceolata*, *Taraxacum officinale*, *Cichorium intybus*, *Achillea millefolium*, *Trifolium repens*
**Indicator value:** Presence suggests fungal network potential. These species are mycorrhizal and can serve as AMF hosts, indicating some fungal biomass persistence
**Source:** Mycorrhizal status databases; Harper et al. — AMF colonization studies
**Confidence:** Medium — mycorrhizal status of these species is well established, but F:B ratio calibration is absent
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Ratio of mycorrhizal to non-mycorrhizal weed species could serve as a rough F:B proxy

### General F:B ratio context for weed interpretation:
- Agricultural soils: typically F:B < 0.5 (bacterial dominant) → weedy annual species dominate
- Grassland soils: F:B ~1:1 → perennial grasses + forbs
- Forest soils: F:B > 2:1 → woody perennials
- **Weed rule of thumb:** High annual weed diversity with Brassicaceae/Chenopodiaceae dominance = bacterial soil; increasing perennial mycorrhizal weed presence = trending toward fungal balance
- Source: Ward Laboratories "Fungal to Bacterial Ratios: What and Why?"; SoilBeat "How to Balance a High Bacterial to Fungal Ratio"; Dr. Elaine Ingham's Soil Food Web research

> **Recommendation:** This dimension needs dedicated field research pairing weed community surveys with phospholipid fatty acid (PLFA) analysis or soil microbiome sequencing to calibrate weed community → F:B ratio mapping. AUGURY could pioneer this calibration.

## Dimension 9: Erosion Status

**Summary:** Several weed species are documented indicators of active soil erosion, leaching, soil instability, or lack of soil cover. This dimension allows AUGURY to distinguish between stable and actively eroding sites.

### Species: *Spergula arvensis* (corn spurry)
**Indicator value:** Active erosion and leaching due to lack of soil cover; "loss of CHC binding power due to deficiency in stable humus / leaching, erosion"; prefers sandy acid soils with low retention capacity
**Source:** Maughan & Amos (2021); Ducerf (2014) — 3-4 corroborating sources
**Confidence:** High — multiple sources specifically note erosion and leaching as indicator values
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Corn spurry = strongest single-species erosion indicator; flags sandy soil losing structure and nutrients

### Species: *Poa annua* (annual meadowgrass)
**Indicator value:** "Erosion and leaching of soils with low retention capacity"; compacted soil; fertile soil
**Source:** Maughan & Amos (2021) — multiple corroborating sources
**Confidence:** Medium — erosion listed alongside other indicators
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Poa annua in compacted areas → surface sealing leading to runoff erosion

### Species: *Polygonum aviculare* (common knotgrass / prostrate knotweed)
**Indicator value:** "Erosion (summer)"; compacted soil; excess nitrates/nitrites on bare soil; low crop cover
**Source:** Maughan & Amos (2021); Ducerf (2014)
**Confidence:** Medium — erosion noted as a seasonal (summer) indicator
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Knotweed on bare compacted ground → surface erosion active in summer rainfall events

### Species: *Senecio vulgaris* (common groundsel)
**Indicator value:** "Loose / low retention power / erosion" in base-rich, high-fertility sandy loam soils; excess nitrogen
**Source:** Maughan & Amos (2021); Ducerf (2014)
**Confidence:** Low-Medium — erosion associated with soil type rather than active erosion per se
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Groundsel in sandy soils → soil vulnerable to erosion due to low retention; manage cover

### Species: *Tussilago farfara* (colt's foot)
**Indicator value:** Soil instability / moving soils (when in base-rich clay conditions); wet/poorly drained; water pockets
**Source:** Maughan & Amos (2021); Ducerf (2014)
**Confidence:** Medium — instability/moving soils explicitly noted
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Colt's foot on slopes → active soil movement / slumping; geotechnical concern

### Species: *Lamium amplexicaule* (henbit dead-nettle)
**Indicator value:** "Erosion and leaching" in base-rich sandy loam soils (as noted in bioindicator guide); nutrient-rich conditions
**Source:** Maughan & Amos (2021); Ducerf (2014)
**Confidence:** Low-Medium — erosion noted but less specifically tied to this species
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Use as community-level erosion signal rather than single-species indicator

### Erosion community interpretation:
- Multiple species from the above list co-occurring → active erosion likely
- Absence of ground cover (noted for Stellaria media: "low crop cover, sparse crops"; Papaver rhoeas: "low cover") combined with erosion indicators → strong erosion signal
- Source: Maughan & Amos (2021) Bioindicators Field Guide; Ducerf (2011-2014)

## Dimension 10: Fire History

**Summary:** Post-fire pioneer species — especially "fire followers" with fire-stimulated seed germination — can indicate recent fire history. While many fire-followers are native wildflowers rather than agricultural weeds, several weedy pioneer species reliably colonize burned areas.

### Species: *Chamerion angustifolium* (fireweed / rosebay willowherb)
**Indicator value:** Recent fire history (within 1-5 years); pioneer colonizer of burned forest and grassland; also colonizes other disturbed sites (roadsides, clear-cuts, construction sites). Deep rhizomes survive fire and rapidly resprout; wind-dispersed seeds colonize from surrounding areas
**Source:** USDA Forest Service FEIS database; Wikipedia; Calscape.org; WNPS "Frenemies—Making Peace with Native Weeds"; WFCA "Ecological Succession After a Forest Fire"
**Confidence:** High — universally recognized as classic post-fire pioneer; common name "fireweed" derives from this association
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Fireweed dominance in non-urban/non-roadsite settings → investigate fire history in last 1-5 years. Also note that fireweed colonizes any mineral soil exposure, so consider alternative disturbance history.

### Species: *Pteridium aquilinum* (bracken fern)
**Indicator value:** Post-fire colonizer; deep rhizomes survive fire; quickly resprouts and dominates post-fire landscapes
**Source:** WFCA "Ecological Succession After a Forest Fire"; National Forest Foundation "How Trees Survive and Thrive After A Fire"
**Confidence:** Medium-High — well-documented fire survivor and post-fire dominant
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Bracken + fireweed = strong post-fire community signal; bracken's deep rhizomes (~1m) indicate fire didn't penetrate deep soil

### Species: Moss (Bryophytes — various species)
**Indicator value:** Early post-fire colonizer; can establish within ~2 months of fire on bare mineral soil; indicates recent fire or other disturbance exposing bare soil
**Source:** WFCA "Ecological Succession After a Forest Fire"
**Confidence:** Medium — mosses colonize many bare substrates, not exclusively post-fire
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Moss + fireweed on otherwise bare soil → very recent fire (<1 year) or similar disturbance

### Species: *Senecio* spp. (groundsels, ragworts) — fire-following annuals
**Indicator value:** Several Senecio species are documented fire-followers; wind-dispersed seeds rapidly colonize burned areas
**Source:** Theodore Payne Foundation "Post-fire Regeneration"; general fire ecology literature
**Confidence:** Low-Medium — genus-level association; many Senecio species are generalist colonizers
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Use as secondary fire indicator; combine with stronger indicators

### Species: *Cytisus scoparius* (Scotch broom — Fabaceae)
**Indicator value:** Fire-adapted invasive; seeds are stimulated by fire and can remain viable in soil seed bank for decades; post-fire nitrogen flush favors its nitrogen-fixing capability
**Source:** National Forest Foundation; USFS fire ecology literature; various invasive species databases
**Confidence:** Medium — well-documented fire-adapted invasive
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Scotch broom explosion after disturbance → consider fire history or soil heating

### General post-fire weed ecology:
- Post-fire nitrogen flush (mineralization of organic N) attracts nitrophilous weeds: *Urtica dioica*, *Chenopodium album*, *Amaranthus* spp.
- Fire removes organic horizon → exposes mineral soil → favors small-seeded annual colonizers
- Fire-released nutrients (P, K, Ca in ash) → brief fertility pulse → rapid weed colonization
- Source: Goodrich (2023), "The post-fire environment: understory plant recovery," NAU; Abella (2009), "Early post-fire plant establishment on a Mojave Desert burn," UNLV

> **Practical note:** Fire history indicators are most reliable in the first 2-3 years post-fire. After that, successional communities merge with general disturbance indicators. Combine with soil carbon data (charcoal layers, reduced OM) for confirmation.

## Dimension 11: Heavy Metal Contamination

**Summary:** Hyperaccumulator plant species can indicate specific heavy metal contamination in soils. Several weedy or ruderal species are documented hyperaccumulators of cadmium, zinc, nickel, lead, arsenic, and other metals. This dimension allows AUGURY to flag potential contamination from weed community composition.

### Zinc / Cadmium Hyperaccumulators:

### Species: *Thlaspi caerulescens* (Alpine pennycress — Brassicaceae)
**Indicator value:** Zn and Cd hyperaccumulator; can accumulate up to 38,000 mg/kg Zn and 1,020 mg/kg Cd in shoots without toxicity. Indicates Zn/Pb-rich soils, serpentine soils, or contaminated sites
**Source:** Milner & Kochian (2008), "Investigating Heavy-metal Hyperaccumulation using Thlaspi caerulescens," *PMC*; Reeves et al. (2018), "A global database for plants that hyperaccumulate metal and metalloid," *New Phytologist*; Wikipedia "Hyperaccumulator"
**Confidence:** Very High — one of the most studied hyperaccumulator model species
**Practical value to AUGURY:** T. caerulescens presence → test for Zn (>10,000 mg/kg likely) and Cd (>100 mg/kg); soil likely needs phytoremediation

### Species: *Arabidopsis halleri* (Brassicaceae)
**Indicator value:** Zn and Cd hyperaccumulator; closely related to model organism A. thaliana; indicates Zn/Cd-contaminated or naturally enriched soils
**Source:** Assunção et al. (2003), review paper; Reeves et al. (2018) global database
**Confidence:** High — well-studied hyperaccumulator
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Similar diagnostic value to T. caerulescens

### Nickel Hyperaccumulators:

### Species: *Alyssum murale*, *Alyssum bertolonii*, *Alyssum markgrafii* (Brassicaceae)
**Indicator value:** Ni hyperaccumulators; indicate ultramafic/serpentine soils naturally enriched in nickel and cobalt; A. murale can accumulate >1% Ni in dry biomass
**Source:** Broadhurst et al. (2016), "Growth and Metal Accumulation of an Alyssum murale..." *Frontiers in Plant Science*; Reeves et al. (2018); Whiting et al. (2003), FAO report
**Confidence:** High — Alyssum species are classic Ni hyperaccumulators
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Alyssum species presence → serpentine/ultramafic geology; elevated Ni, Co, Cr; low Ca:Mg ratio; specialized plant community

### Species: *Berkheya coddii* (Asteraceae — South African)
**Indicator value:** Ni hyperaccumulator; indicates serpentine soils in southern Africa
**Source:** Mesjasz-Przybyłowicz et al. (2004); Reeves et al. (2018) — listed under Asteraceae Ni hyperaccumulators
**Confidence:** High — well-documented
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Regional indicator (southern Africa)

### Lead / Multi-metal Accumulators:

### Species: *Brassica juncea* (Indian mustard)
**Indicator value:** Pb, Cd, Cr, Ni, Zn accumulator; high biomass (~8 t/ha in 2 months); deep root system; indicates multi-metal contamination
**Source:** Biotechnologia Journal review paper; Alluvial Soil Lab "Using Common Plants to Remove Heavy Metals from Garden Soils"
**Confidence:** High — extensively studied for phytoremediation
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Volunteer B. juncea on urban/industrial sites → test for Pb, Cd, Zn contamination

### Species: *Helianthus annuus* (common sunflower)
**Indicator value:** Pb accumulator; also takes up various metals; large biomass enables significant Pb removal per growing season
**Source:** Alluvial Soil Lab; various phytoremediation studies; USDA ARS (1995), "Cleaning up with Plants"
**Confidence:** Medium — well-documented for phytoremediation but less specific as a wild indicator
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Volunteer sunflowers on disturbed urban soil → test for Pb (legacy paint, gasoline)

### Species: *Ambrosia artemisiifolia* (common ragweed)
**Indicator value:** Pb accumulator; USDA ARS research identified ragweed as effective for Pb removal
**Source:** USDA ARS (1995), "Cleaning up with Plants," *Agricultural Research* magazine
**Confidence:** Medium — noted in USDA research
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Ragweed in urban/industrial wasteland → Pb contamination possible

### Arsenic Hyperaccumulators:

### Species: *Pteris vittata* (Chinese brake fern)
**Indicator value:** Arsenic hyperaccumulator; stores exceptionally high As levels in fronds; indicates As-contaminated soil (natural or anthropogenic)
**Source:** Alluvial Soil Lab; various phytoremediation studies
**Confidence:** Very High — first discovered and most studied As hyperaccumulator
**Practical value to AUGURY:** P. vittata presence → test for arsenic (former orchard sites, industrial areas, CCA-treated wood sites)

### Invasive Weed Hyperaccumulators:

### Species: *Parthenium hysterophorus* (parthenium weed), *Alternanthera philoxeroides* (alligator weed)
**Indicator value:** Both invasive species show significant potential for Cd and Zn accumulation in contaminated soils; highly adaptable to stressed environments
**Source:** CWE Journal, "Evaluation of Weed Plant Species Potential for Phytoremediation in Heavy Metal Contaminated Soil"
**Confidence:** Low-Medium — documented accumulation but not as primary hyperaccumulators
**Practical value to AUGURY:** These invasive weeds on degraded/industrial sites → test for heavy metals; their presence may indicate contamination that excludes less-tolerant species

### Hyperaccumulator Interpretation Framework:
- **Serpentine indicator community:** Alyssum spp. + stunted vegetation + low diversity = ultramafic soils with high Ni, Cr, Co
- **Mine/smelter indicator community:** Thlaspi caerulescens + metal-tolerant grasses = Zn/Pb/Cd contamination from mining/smelting
- **Urban contamination:** Brassica juncea + Helianthus annuus + Ambrosia on disturbed sites = Pb, Cd, Zn from legacy pollution
- **Note:** Many hyperaccumulators are Brassicaceae — combine with "soil biology" dimension to infer both contamination AND disrupted mycorrhizal networks

## Dimension 12: Salinity Type (Coastal vs Inland/Dryland)

**Summary:** Halophytic (salt-tolerant) weed communities can be distinguished between coastal/marine-influenced salinity and inland/dryland salinity. This dimension adds salinity-type specificity beyond the current binary "salinity" dimension.

### Coastal / Marine Salinity Indicators:

### Species: *Salicornia europaea* (common glasswort / marsh samphire)
**Indicator value:** Coastal or inland saline habitats; extremely salt-tolerant (up to seawater salinity ~500 mM NaCl); obligate halophyte. In coastal settings, indicates regular marine inundation or salt spray zone
**Source:** Piernik et al. (2025), "Functional Trait Variability of Salicornia europaea," *Plants* MDPI; Frontiers in Plant Science (2023), "Halophytes as new model plant species"; Piernik (2003), "Inland halophilous vegetation as indicator of soil salinity"
**Confidence:** Very High — the archetypal halophyte indicator
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Salicornia presence → salinity at or above seawater levels (EC >40 dS/m); if coastal → marine influence; if inland → extreme dryland salinity

### Species: *Suaeda* spp. (seepweeds / sea blites), *Sarcocornia* spp.
**Indicator value:** Coastal saline habitats; upper salt marsh indicators; tolerate fluctuating salinity; Chenopodiaceae family
**Source:** Frontiers in Plant Science (2023); Biosaline.org "Salt-Tolerant Plants of the UAE"; Meinzer (1927)
**Confidence:** High — well-documented coastal halophyte genera
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Suaeda + Salicornia community → coastal salt marsh or highly saline inland basin

### Species: *Atriplex portulacoides* (sea purslane), *Atriplex halimus* (Mediterranean saltbush)
**Indicator value:** Coastal halophytes; A. halimus shows distinct coastal vs inland population differences in salt tolerance mechanisms
**Source:** Hassine et al. (2008), "An inland and a coastal population of Atriplex halimus differ in their ability to accumulate proline..." *Journal of Experimental Botany*
**Confidence:** Medium-High — well-studied coastal halophytes with documented population differentiation
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Atriplex species identification to ecotype level could distinguish coastal from inland salinity

### Species: *Spartina* spp. (cordgrasses), *Puccinellia* spp. (alkali grasses)
**Indicator value:** Coastal salt marsh and inland alkali flat indicators; Puccinellia occurs in both coastal and inland saline settings
**Source:** USFWS Tamarix documentation (Puccinellia); general coastal ecology literature
**Confidence:** Medium-High — well-known salt marsh genera
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Spartina → coastal; Puccinellia → could be either; combine with other indicators

### Inland / Dryland Salinity Indicators:

### Species: *Salsola kali* (Russian thistle / tumbleweed)
**Indicator value:** Inland/dryland saline and disturbed soils; iconic tumbleweed of arid western North America; Chenopodiaceae family — non-mycorrhizal, salt-tolerant colonizer
**Source:** General weed ecology literature; Biosaline.org; USDA plant guides
**Confidence:** Medium — widely recognized but primarily as a disturbance indicator rather than specifically salinity
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Russian thistle in arid/semi-arid regions → test for dryland salinity, especially in lower landscape positions

### Species: *Kochia scoparia* (summer cypress / burning bush / fireweed — not to be confused with Chamerion)
**Indicator value:** Inland saline soil indicator; Chenopodiaceae; highly salt-tolerant annual; common on saline roadsides and disturbed dryland sites
**Source:** USDA FEIS Tamarix documentation (listed in alkali sink vegetation with saltcedar); general weed ecology
**Confidence:** Medium — recognized halophyte in inland settings
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Kochia dominance → inland salinity or sodic soil conditions

### Species: *Tamarix* spp. (saltcedar — multiple invasive species)
**Indicator value:** Facultative phreatophyte; increases surface soil salinity through leaf litter salt excretion; indicates accessible deep groundwater; associated with inland riparian salinity in arid regions
**Source:** USFWS Ecological Risk Screening Summary; USDA FEIS; Texas Invasives; Weld County Weed Management
**Confidence:** High — salinity association is a defining characteristic of this invasive genus
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Tamarix presence flags both accessible groundwater AND increasing surface salinity; distinguishes from coastal saline sites (Tamarix is inland)

### Species: *Sarcobatus vermiculatus* (black greasewood)
**Indicator value:** Inland saline/alkaline basin indicator; phreatophyte; indicates moderately deep water table and elevated salinity in arid/semi-arid basins of western North America
**Source:** Meinzer (1927); USDA FEIS Tamarix documentation
**Confidence:** High — classic indicator of inland alkaline/saline basins
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Greasewood + saltgrass → inland dryland salinity with accessible groundwater

### Species: *Atriplex canescens* (fourwing saltbush), *Atriplex confertifolia* (shadscale)
**Indicator value:** Inland dryland salinity indicators; phreatophytic or facultative phreatophytes; tolerant of both salinity and drought
**Source:** Meinzer (1927); USDA FEIS
**Confidence:** Medium-High — well-documented inland halophytes
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Atriplex spp. in non-coastal arid settings → dryland salinity

### Species: *Distichlis spicata* (inland saltgrass), *Halogeton glomeratus* (halogeton)
**Indicator value:** Inland saline/alkaline soil indicators; saltgrass widespread in both coastal and inland saline settings; halogeton is an invasive annual halophyte of disturbed saline drylands
**Source:** Meinzer (1927) — saltgrass listed under "Salt grass" in phreatophyte survey; USDA weed databases
**Confidence:** Medium (saltgrass), Low-Medium (halogeton)
**Practical value to AUGURY:** Saltgrass = saline soil confirmation; halogeton = disturbed inland saline sites

### Salinity Type Differentiation Framework:
| Feature | Coastal Salinity | Inland/Dryland Salinity |
|---------|-----------------|------------------------|
| Dominant species | Salicornia, Suaeda, Spartina, Sarcocornia | Salsola, Kochia, Sarcobatus, Atriplex, Tamarix |
| Sodium source | Marine inundation, salt spray | Evaporative concentration, saline groundwater discharge |
| Associated water | Tidal influence, shallow marine groundwater | Deep saline groundwater, ephemeral surface water |
| Soil texture | Often sandy/silty (marine sediments) | Often clayey (playas, basins) |
| pH range | Near-neutral to slightly alkaline | Often highly alkaline (sodic) |
| Distinguishing species | Obligate coastal halophytes (Salicornia europaea coastal ecotype) | Inland basin phreatophytes (Sarcobatus, greasewood) |
