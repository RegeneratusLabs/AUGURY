## Executive Summary

Weeds do not exist in isolation. They function as nodes in complex ecological networks, connecting soil conditions to insects, fungi, microbes, livestock health, hydrology, and climate dynamics. This track documents six adjacent-domain connections that extend AUGURY's interpretive power beyond soil chemistry into whole-farm ecosystem health.

---

## 1. Weed ↔ Insect Connections

### What Was Found

Weeds serve dual roles as both **beneficial insect habitat** and **pest reservoirs**. Flowering weeds provide nectar, pollen, and shelter for predatory and parasitic insects (natural enemies), while also acting as alternate hosts for crop pests during off-seasons. The net effect depends on species identity, proximity to crops, and management context.

Key findings:
- Weeds with umbel flowers (Apiaceae) and composite flowers (Asteraceae) are disproportionately valuable for parasitoid wasps, hoverflies, and lacewings.
- Broadleaf weeds near orchards can harbor spider mites; removing them reduces pest pressure (NC State Extension).
- Weeds serve as "bridge" hosts for whitefly (*Bemisia tabaci*), aphids, and thrips during crop-free periods (USU Extension).
- Carabid ground beetles — which overwinter in weedy field margins — are major weed seed predators, reducing the weed seed bank.
- Shepherds purse (*Capsella bursa-pastoris*), wild mustard, and dandelion are documented attractors of tachinid flies, syrphid flies, and bees.

### Species Involved

| Weed Species | Insect Connection | Role |
|---|---|---|
| **Dandelion** (*Taraxacum officinale*) | Early-season nectar/pollen for bees, hoverflies, lady beetles | Beneficial insect support |
| **Shepherd's purse** (*Capsella bursa-pastoris*) | Attracts tachinid flies, syrphid flies, parasitic wasps | Natural enemy habitat |
| **Wild mustard** (*Sinapis arvensis*) | Nectar source for parasitoid wasps; also hosts harlequin bugs | Mixed (beneficial + pest) |
| **Queen Anne's lace** (*Daucus carota*) | Umbel flowers attract parasitic mini-wasps, hoverflies, lacewings | Beneficial insectary plant |
| **Lambsquarters** (*Chenopodium album*) | Alternate host for whitefly, aphids, leaf miners | Pest reservoir |
| **Redroot pigweed** (*Amaranthus retroflexus*) | Hosts lygus bugs, flea beetles, stink bugs | Pest reservoir |

### Sources
- UC IPM (University of California): "Biological Control and Natural Enemies" — ipm.ucanr.edu
- NC State Extension: "Integrated Pest Management" — content.ces.ncsu.edu
- Cornell University Biocontrol: "Weed-feeders" — biocontrol.entomology.cornell.edu
- Washington State University Extension: "Gardening with Companion Plants"
- Permies forum: documented shepherd's purse as tachinid/syrphid attractor

### Confidence: **HIGH**
Multiple university extension sources confirm these relationships. The beneficial-insect-supporting role of flowering weeds is well-established in IPM literature.

### Practical Value to AUGURY
AUGURY could classify a weed not just by soil indication but by **entomological function**: "This dandelion population is providing early-season beneficial insect habitat — removal should be timed after primary pollinator emergence." Weed community composition near crop margins could predict natural enemy vs. pest pressure.

## 2. Weed ↔ Fungal Connections

### What Was Found

Arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) fungi form symbioses with ~80% of vascular plant species, including most agricultural weeds. Recent research (Trinchera et al., 2023; Veiga et al., 2011) demonstrates that **AM-host weeds can participate in and strengthen common mycorrhizal networks (CMNs)** that connect crops to soil fungal infrastructure.

Key findings:
- Most agricultural weeds show >50% AMF root colonization (Veiga et al., 2011 — *PLOS ONE*).
- Weeds can serve as "AMF reservoirs" during fallow periods, maintaining fungal inoculum for subsequent crops.
- AM-host weeds can participate in common mycorrhizal mycelial networks (MMN) that transfer nutrients between species (Trinchera et al., 2023 — *Microorganisms*).
- Non-mycorrhizal weed families (Brassicaceae, Chenopodiaceae) indicate soil disturbance and low AMF network integrity.
- Natural fallow (N treatment) maintained 42% higher AMF biomass than physical clearance, 35% higher than deep tillage, and 91% higher than herbicide treatment (PMC 6733240).
- Soil-borne fungal pathogens (*Fusarium*, *Rhizoctonia*) interact synergistically with root-feeding insects on weeds like leafy spurge (*Euphorbia esula*) — USDA ARS research.

### Species Involved

| Weed Species | Fungal Connection | Role |
|---|---|---|
| **Amaranthus spp.** (pigweeds) | >50% AMF colonization; strong mycorrhizal hosts | AMF network contributor |
| **Lambsquarters** (*Chenopodium album*) | Non-mycorrhizal (Chenopodiaceae); may benefit from CMNs of neighbors | AMF absence indicator |
| **Wild mustard** (*Sinapis arvensis*) | Non-mycorrhizal (Brassicaceae); produces glucosinolates antifungal | Fungal suppressant |
| **Leafy spurge** (*Euphorbia esula*) | Synergistic interaction with soil pathogens *Fusarium* + *Rhizoctonia* | Pathogen host complex |
| **Plantago spp.** (plantains) | Strong AMF host; common in low-disturbance soils | AMF network indicator |
| **Dandelion** (*Taraxacum officinale*) | Strong AMF colonizer | AMF reservoir |

### Sources
- Trinchera et al. (2023): "Weeds: An Insidious Enemy or a Tool to Boost Mycorrhization in Cropping Systems" — *Microorganisms* 11(2):334
- Veiga et al. (2011): "Can Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Fungi Reduce the Growth of Agricultural Weeds?" — *PLOS ONE*
- Science (2026): "Global density and biomass of arbuscular mycorrhizal fungal networks" — *Science*
- Chauhan et al. (2025): CMN effects on non-mycorrhizal *Chenopodium album* — *PubMed* 39837592
- USDA ARS: "Soilborne microorganisms of Euphorbia" — pathogen-weed-insect synergism

### Confidence: **MEDIUM-HIGH**
The basic biology of weed-AMF associations is well-established. The functional significance of weeds as AMF reservoirs and CMN participants is supported by recent peer-reviewed research but is still an emerging area.

### Practical Value to AUGURY
AUGURY could assess **mycorrhizal network integrity** from weed community composition. A shift from AM-host weeds (pigweed, dandelion, plantain) toward non-mycorrhizal weeds (mustards, lambsquarters) signals declining fungal network health — a powerful soil health indicator invisible to chemical tests alone. This connects directly to Track 3 (soil chemistry) and Track 2 (weed indicators).

## 3. Weed ↔ Microbial Connections

### What Was Found

Weeds shape their rhizosphere microbiome distinctly from crops and from each other, with measurable effects on soil bacterial and fungal community composition. Plants actively recruit protective microbial communities through root exudates.

Key findings:
- Five common weed species (*Digitaria sanguinalis*, *Echinochloa crusgalli*, *Acalypha australis*, *Portulaca oleracea*, *Chenopodium album*) all had significantly lower rhizosphere bacterial and fungal Shannon diversity compared to alfalfa (*Medicago sativa*) in the same field (Yang et al., 2022 — *Frontiers in Microbiology*).
- Weeds significantly decreased *Sphingomonadaceae* and *Chitinophagaceae* (beneficial bacterial families) while increasing *Acidobacteriaceae* and *Rhodanobacteraceae* compared to crop rhizospheres.
- Different weed species recruit distinct microbial taxa — plants shape their "second genome" through root exudate chemistry (Berendsen et al., 2012).
- Weed management method dramatically affects soil microbial biomass: natural fallow maintained highest AMF biomass; herbicide treatment produced lowest total bacterial, fungal, and actinomycete biomass.
- The rhizosphere microbiome functions as a protective barrier; beneficial microbes can induce systemic resistance (ISR) in plants against pathogens.

### Species Involved

| Weed Species | Microbial Connection | Role |
|---|---|---|
| **Crabgrass** (*Digitaria sanguinalis*) | Lower rhizosphere bacterial diversity than alfalfa; distinct community composition | Microbial community shaper |
| **Purslane** (*Portulaca oleracea*) | Hosts distinct bacterial assemblages; succulent physiology affects rhizosphere | Microbial community shaper |
| **Lambsquarters** (*Chenopodium album*) | Decreased *Sphingomonadaceae*; increased *Acidobacteriaceae* | Microbial indicator |
| **Barnyard grass** (*Echinochloa crusgalli*) | Distinct rhizosphere fungal composition correlated with soil TP and PO₄³⁻-P | Nutrient-microbe link |
| **Leguminous weeds** (clovers, vetches) | Host *Rhizobium* spp. for nitrogen fixation; enrich N-cycling bacteria | Beneficial N-fixers |

### Sources
- Yang et al. (2022): "Weeds in the Alfalfa Field Decrease Rhizosphere Microbial Diversity" — *Frontiers in Microbiology* 13:840774
- Berendsen, Pieterse & Bakker (2012): "The rhizosphere microbiome and plant health" — *Trends in Plant Science*
- Monteiro et al. (2024): "Rhizosphere microbial community changes due to weed..." — *ScienceDirect* S1164556323001309
- PMC 6733240: "Responses of weed community, soil nutrients, and microbes to different weed management practices"
- UF/IFAS SL510/SS723: "Rhizosphere Microbes and the Roles They Play in Crop Production"

### Confidence: **MEDIUM**
Research on weed-specific rhizosphere microbiomes in agricultural contexts is growing rapidly. The core finding — that weed species differ from crops in their microbiome recruitment — is supported by multiple studies.

### Practical Value to AUGURY
AUGURY could infer **rhizosphere microbiome shifts** from weed species presence. Dominance of certain weeds may signal that the soil microbial community has shifted in ways that favor those weeds over crops — a self-reinforcing feedback loop. This connects microbial health directly to weed management strategy selection.

## 4. Weed ↔ Livestock Connections

### What Was Found

Weeds in pastures function as both **mineral deficiency indicators** and **direct toxicity hazards** to livestock. The relationship is bidirectional: soil mineral imbalances promote specific weeds, and those weeds either provide or deny essential minerals to grazers.

Key findings:
- **Calcium deficiency** promotes bitterweed (*Helenium tenuifolium*), horsetail (*Equisetum arvense*), stinging nettle (*Urtica dioica*), wild buckwheat, and curly dock (MSU Extension).
- **Low phosphorus** promotes lambsquarters, knapweed, oxeye daisy, and wild buckwheat.
- **Compacted, poorly drained soils** promote curly dock, foxtail barley, and horsetail — classic pasture degradation indicators.
- **Pyrrolizidine alkaloid (PA) weeds** — *Senecio jacobaea* (tansy ragwort), *Crotalaria* spp., *Amsinckia* spp., *Cynoglossum officinale* (houndstongue), *Echium* spp. — cause cumulative, often fatal liver damage in cattle, horses, and pigs. PAs are the most important natural toxins affecting Australian livestock.
- **Oxalate toxicity** from curly dock (*Rumex crispus*) and pigweed (*Amaranthus* spp.) binds calcium, causing kidney damage in sheep and cattle.
- **Nitrate-accumulating weeds** (pigweed, lambsquarters, nightshade) can cause acute nitrate poisoning, especially after drought or herbicide application.
- **Magnesium deficiency** in lush spring pastures (grass tetany) is exacerbated by potassium fertilization — weed indicator species can help predict risk zones.

### Species Involved

| Weed Species | Livestock Connection | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| **Tansy ragwort** (*Senecio jacobaea*) | Cumulative liver failure (pyrrolizidine alkaloids); horses most sensitive | Hepatotoxic |
| **Curly dock** (*Rumex crispus*) | Oxalate toxicity; kidney damage in cattle/sheep/poultry | Oxalate poisoning |
| **Horsetail** (*Equisetum arvense*) | Thiaminase enzyme destroys vitamin B1 in horses; calcium deficiency indicator | Anti-nutrient |
| **Redroot pigweed** (*Amaranthus retroflexus*) | Nitrate accumulation; oxalate toxicity; low Ca/high K soil indicator | Nitrate + oxalate |
| **Stinging nettle** (*Urtica dioica*) | Calcium deficiency indicator; high mineral content when dried (fed as forage) | Mixed (indicator + feed) |
| **Bitterweed** (*Helenium tenuifolium*) | Calcium/phosphorus imbalance indicator; toxic sesquiterpene lactones | Toxic + indicator |

### Sources
- MSU Extension: "Weeds are an indicator of a soil's health" — canr.msu.edu (Dixie Sandborn, 2019)
- USDA ARS Poisonous Plant Research: "Tansy Ragwort" — ars.usda.gov
- Oregon State University Extension: "Pyrrolizidine Alkaloids" and "Management Strategies for Poisonous Plants"
- Oklahoma State University: "Vitamin and Mineral Nutrition of Grazing Cattle"
- Australian Veterinarians Toxicology: pyrrolizidine alkaloids in rattlepods, heliotrope
- Colorado Arts & Sciences: "Curly dock has all the traits of a super weed"

### Confidence: **HIGH**
Decades of veterinary toxicology research and extension publications firmly establish these weed-livestock connections. Mineral deficiency-weed indicator relationships are widely used in agronomy.

### Practical Value to AUGURY
This is one of the highest-value connections for AUGURY. By identifying toxic and indicator weeds in pasture imagery, AUGURY could:
1. Alert graziers to **PA-containing weeds before hay cutting** (PA-contaminated hay is the primary poisoning route).
2. Map **mineral deficiency zones** from weed species distribution — e.g., calcium-deficient zones indicated by horsetail/dock/nettle prevalence.
3. Predict **grass tetany risk** from weed community + seasonal + fertilization data.

## 5. Weed ↔ Water Connections

### What Was Found

Weed species composition is a reliable indicator of **water table depth, drainage quality, and wetland hydrology**. Phreatophytic weeds access groundwater directly; wetland-obligate weeds indicate saturated soil conditions.

Key findings:
- **Phreatophytes** (deep-rooted plants accessing groundwater): salt cedar (*Tamarix* spp., roots >30 m), Russian olive (*Elaeagnus angustifolia*), cottonwood. Their presence reliably indicates accessible water table within rooting depth (USGS, Kansas Geological Survey).
- **Field horsetail** (*Equisetum arvense*) is a classic indicator of poorly drained, low-pH soils with a high water table. High silica content in stems correlates with groundwater access (WSU Extension).
- **Wetland indicator species** follow clear hydrologic zonation: *Typha* spp. (cattails) dominate at 0–100 cm water depth; *Phragmites australis* (common reed) tolerates lower water tables and disturbed conditions.
- Cattails show higher photosynthetic resilience to varying water depth; reeds show clearer zonation (Song, 2021 — *Photosynthetica*).
- Non-native *Phragmites australis* invasion is a strong indicator of **disturbed wetland hydrology** (Chambers et al., 1999).
- **Curly dock** (*Rumex crispus*) is "frequently associated with overwatering or standing water in low areas" (USU Extension).
- **Foxtail barley** (*Hordeum jubatum*) indicates compacted, poorly drained soil — a water infiltration problem.
- US Army Corps of Engineers wetland delineation uses vegetation as a primary hydrology indicator (hydrophytic vegetation).

### Species Involved

| Weed Species | Water Connection | What It Indicates |
|---|---|---|
| **Field horsetail** (*Equisetum arvense*) | High silica from groundwater; thrives in poorly drained, high water table soils | Shallow water table; poor drainage |
| **Cattail** (*Typha latifolia*, *T. angustifolia*) | Obligate wetland; optimal growth in 0–100 cm standing water | Permanent saturation; wetland hydrology |
| **Common reed** (*Phragmites australis*) | Tolerates variable water tables; invasive in disturbed wetlands | Disturbed hydrology; water table fluctuation |
| **Curly dock** (*Rumex crispus*) | Deep taproot; associated with overwatering/standing water | Seasonal saturation; compacted wet soils |
| **Foxtail barley** (*Hordeum jubatum*) | Compacted, poorly drained, high-magnesium soils | Surface waterlogging |
| **Salt cedar** (*Tamarix* spp.) | Phreatophyte; roots reach >30 m to water table | Deep water table presence; salinization risk |

### Sources
- Kansas Geological Survey: "Phreatophytes: Plants That Suck Up Groundwater" — geokansas.ku.edu
- USGS Publications: "Phreatophytes" — usgs.gov
- Song (2021): "Comparison of water depth tolerance in *Phragmites australis* and *Typha angustifolia*" — *Photosynthetica*
- Washington State University: "Horsetail" — high silica linked to water table
- USU Extension: "Curly Dock" — associated with overwatering
- USACE Wetlands Delineation Manual: hydrology indicators
- Springer: "Typha Invasion in North American Wetlands" — *Wetlands* (2019)

### Confidence: **HIGH**
Phreatophyte and wetland indicator concepts are mature, well-documented, and used in regulatory wetland delineation.

### Practical Value to AUGURY
AUGURY could use weed community composition to:
1. Map **water table depth zones** without drilling — phreatophyte presence = groundwater within root reach.
2. Identify **drainage problem areas** in fields from horsetail/curly dock/foxtail barley distribution.
3. Monitor **wetland hydrology changes** from *Phragmites* vs. *Typha* dominance shifts — an early warning of altered water regimes.
4. Detect **salinization risk** from salt cedar encroachment.

## 6. Weed ↔ Climate Connections

### What Was Found

Climate change is fundamentally reshaping weed communities through **range expansion, phenological shifts, and altered competitive dynamics**. Weeds — particularly invasive species — are often better positioned than crops or native plants to exploit climate disruption.

Key findings:
- **Kudzu** (*Pueraria montana* var. *lobata*): northern range limited by frost intolerance; with warming, projected to expand northward. Listed among world's 100 worst invasive species. Major isoprene emitter affecting air quality (IPPC).
- **Purple loosestrife** (*Lythrum salicaria*): bioclimatic envelope modeling projects range expansion with climate change; already clogs wetlands across North America (USGS Open-File Report 2006-1153).
- **Cogongrass** (*Imperata cylindrica*), **Chinese privet** (*Ligustrum sinense*): projected climate habitat expansion in southeastern US by 2100 (Bradley et al., 2010).
- Rising CO₂ disproportionately benefits invasive plants over natives (Columbia Climate School, 2024).
- Warming winters facilitate northward range expansion of tropical invasive species from Florida into southeastern US (USGS).
- In Queensland, Australia, climate-induced range shift analysis of 12 emerging weeds shows *Opuntia elata* projected to expand 297% relative to current range (Osunkoya et al., 2025).
- Rapid evolution documented in invasive weeds under climate change: *Reynoutria* spp. (knotweeds) thrive in areas with >500 mm annual precipitation and minimum temperature >−30.2°C; reproductive strategy shifts from outcrossing to selfing in invaded ranges (Clements & DiTommaso, 2011).
- A UMass study of 144 eastern US plants found 2°C warming shifts ranges northeast by ~213 km, exacerbating up to 40 invasive plants' impacts.
- "Sleeper weeds" — previously climate-limited species that may become invasive as conditions change — are an emerging concern.

### Species Involved

| Weed Species | Climate Connection | What It Indicates |
|---|---|---|
| **Kudzu** (*Pueraria montana* var. *lobata*) | Frost-limited; warming winters = northward range expansion | Climate warming indicator; range shift |
| **Purple loosestrife** (*Lythrum salicaria*) | Wetland invader; range expansion with warmer/wetter conditions | Hydrologic + climate change |
| **Cogongrass** (*Imperata cylindrica*) | Projected range expansion in SE US by 2100 | Climate envelope shift |
| **Prickly pear** (*Opuntia elata*) | 297% range expansion projected in Queensland under climate change | Arid-zone climate indicator |
| **Japanese knotweed** (*Reynoutria japonica*) | Requires >500 mm precip, min temp >−30.2°C; sexually adaptive | Climate niche indicator |
| **Chinese privet** (*Ligustrum sinense*) | Expanding climate habitat in SE US under warming scenarios | Subtropical expansion indicator |

### Sources
- Bradley et al. (2010): "Climate change increases risk of plant invasion in the Eastern US" — *Biological Invasions*
- Bradley (2014): "Projecting Range Shifts with Climate Change" — Chapter 15, *Invasive Species and Climate Change*
- USGS: "Plant migration due to winter climate change" — usgs.gov
- IPPC: "The impact of climate change on kudzu" — ippc.int
- Osunkoya et al. (2025): "Climate-Induced Range Shift and Risk Assessment of Emerging Weeds in Queensland" — *Ecology and Evolution*
- Frontiers in Agronomy (2021): "Rapid Evolution of Invasive Weeds Under Climate Change"
- Columbia Climate School (2024): "How Climate Change Drives the Spread of Invasive Plants"

### Confidence: **MEDIUM-HIGH**
Species distribution modeling under climate scenarios is well-established, but projections carry uncertainty from model choice and emission scenarios. The directional trend (poleward/upward expansion) is highly robust.

### Practical Value to AUGURY
AUGURY could integrate climate projection layers to:
1. Forecast **which weed species are likely to appear** in a region within 10–20 years — enabling proactive management.
2. Identify **"sleeper weed" risks** — species currently limited by climate that will become problematic as conditions shift.
3. Track **range-edge populations** as sentinels of climate-driven community change.
4. Distinguish climate-driven weed shifts from soil-management-driven shifts — critical for correct diagnosis.

## Cross-Cutting Synthesis

### The Weed as Ecological Node

A single weed species can simultaneously signal multiple adjacent domain conditions:

| Weed Species | Soil Signal | Insect Role | Fungal Role | Livestock Risk | Water Signal |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Curly dock** (*Rumex crispus*) | Compacted, low Ca, high Mg | Hosts crop pathogens | AMF host | Oxalate toxicity | Overwatering/standing water |
| **Horsetail** (*Equisetum arvense*) | Low pH, low Ca | Limited | AMF host | Thiaminase (horses) | High water table |
| **Lambsquarters** (*Chenopodium album*) | Low P, high K | Whitefly/aphid host | Non-mycorrhizal | Nitrate accumulation | Well-drained (opposite of wetland) |
| **Redroot pigweed** (*Amaranthus retroflexus*) | High K, low P, low Ca | Lygus/stink bug host | Strong AMF host (>50%) | Nitrate + oxalate | Adapted to disturbance |
| **Dandelion** (*Taraxacum officinale*) | Compacted, low Ca | Beneficial early nectar/pollen | Strong AMF host | Non-toxic forage | Deep taproot; moderate drainage |

### Network Effects

The most powerful AUGURY insights come not from single-species indicators but from **community-level patterns**:

1. **AMF network health**: Ratio of AM-host weeds (dandelion, pigweed, plantain) to non-mycorrhizal weeds (mustard, lambsquarters) → proxy for fungal network integrity.
2. **Drainage gradient**: Horsetail → curly dock → foxtail barley → dandelion → lambsquarters (wettest to driest).
3. **Livestock risk score**: Presence of PA weeds + oxalate weeds + nitrate weeds → composite pasture toxicity risk.
4. **Climate vulnerability index**: Presence of range-edge invasive species + "sleeper" weeds → early warning of climate-driven community shift.

### Data Gaps Identified

1. **Quantitative thresholds**: How much AMF-host weed cover is needed to meaningfully maintain mycorrhizal networks through fallow?
2. **Microbiome specificity**: Most weed rhizosphere studies examine single species — community-level microbiome effects of mixed weed stands are understudied.
3. **Livestock mineral-weed feedback**: Do high-mineral-accumulator weeds actually improve livestock mineral status when grazed, or does soil deficiency override plant uptake?
4. **Climate-weed validation**: Few long-term field studies validate species distribution model projections against observed weed community shifts.
