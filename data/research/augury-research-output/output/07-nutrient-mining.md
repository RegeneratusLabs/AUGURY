## Section 1: Classic Reference Books — Nutrient Claims

### 1.1 Ehrenfried Pfeiffer — *Weeds and What They Tell* (1950s)

Pfeiffer's biodynamic classic is the foundational text. Specific claims:

#### Clover (*Trifolium* spp.) — Magnesium, Calcium, Nitrogen
**Relationship:** High in magnesium, low in calcium and nitrogen (according to Pfeiffer via Modern Farmer)
**Source:** Pfeiffer, E. *Weeds and What They Tell Us* — cited in Modern Farmer (2019)
**Confidence:** Medium (classic reference, but specific claims from secondary summary)
**Notes:** White/red clover fixes nitrogen but Pfeiffer says its *presence* indicates Mg excess and Ca/N deficiency

#### Dandelion (*Taraxacum officinale*) — Potassium, Calcium  
**Relationship:** High in potassium, low in calcium (Pfeiffer)
**Source:** Modern Farmer summary; LSU AgCenter
**Confidence:** Medium
**Notes:** Dandelion accumulates Ca, K, Fe, Mg, Mn, P, Se, Zn. Its presence often indicates compacted soil with low calcium.

#### Yarrow (*Achillea millefolium*) — Potassium
**Relationship:** Indicates low potassium
**Source:** Pfeiffer, cited in UC ANR "What Weeds Can Tell Us" (2019)
**Confidence:** Low (single secondary source)
**Notes:** Yarrow also cited in bioindicators field guide as indicating low K.

#### Sorrel (*Rumex acetosella*) — Acid soil indicator
**Relationship:** Acidic soils, low calcium
**Source:** Pfeiffer, cited in UC ANR
**Confidence:** Medium

#### Horsetail (*Equisetum arvense*) — Silica, Acid soil
**Relationship:** Indicates acidic, poorly drained soil; accumulates silica
**Source:** Pfeiffer; MSU Extension; multiple sources
**Confidence:** High (for silica accumulation and acid soil)
**Quantitative data:** Horsetail stems contain up to 70% silica ash; accumulates cobalt
**Notes:** Silica accumulation is well-documented. Also accumulates heavy metals from soil.

#### Plantain (*Plantago* spp.) — Potassium
**Relationship:** Rich in potassium; indicates low calcium (narrow-leaf)
**Source:** Pfeiffer via UC ANR
**Confidence:** Medium
**Notes:** Broadleaf plantain indicates high calcium per some sources; narrow-leaf indicates low calcium.

#### Knotweed (*Polygonum* spp.) — Acid soil
**Relationship:** Found in acidic soils
**Source:** Pfeiffer, cited UC ANR
**Confidence:** Medium
**Notes:** Also indicates compacted soil.

### 1.3 Joseph Cocannouer — *Weeds: Guardians of the Soil* (1950)

**Key claims from the PDF text:**
**Source:** Cocannouer, J.A. *Weeds: Guardians of the Soil*, The Devin-Adair Company (1950)
**Confidence:** Medium (pioneering work, observational rather than experimental)

Core thesis: Weeds bring minerals (especially depleted ones) from deep subsoil to surface.

Specific services of weeds (from PDF):
- Bring minerals from deep subsoil, particularly those depleted from surface
- Make minerals readily available 
- Store high-quality phosphorus (specifically goosefoot/lambsquarters)
- Make good eating — high nutrient density
- Nitrogen fixation and preservation
- Soil structure improvement via taproots

## Section 3: Peer-Reviewed Tissue Analysis Papers

### 3.1 Harper, Daniel & Murphy (1933) — Oklahoma classic

**Citation:** Harper, H.J., Daniel, H.A. & Murphy, H.F. "The Total Nitrogen, Phosphorus, and Calcium Content of Common Weeds and Native Grasses in Oklahoma." *Proceedings of the Oklahoma Academy of Science* (1933).

**Key findings:**
- 229 composite samples, 59 species analyzed
- Kling (1914): German literature survey — six common weeds averaged: **N 3.64%, P 0.52%, Ca 2.35%, K 6.20%**
- Korsmo (1930): 53 weeds averaged: **N 2.47%, P 0.33%, K 3.29%, Ca 1.79%**
- Snyder (1907): *Portulaca oleracea* (purslane) = **4.40% nitrogen** (highest in study)
- Weeds consistently higher than grasses in N, P, Ca
- Young plants > old plants; leaves > stems
- Soil quality affects tissue concentrations

**Species-specific data (1933, blooming stage):**

| Species | % N | % P | % Ca |
|---------|-----|-----|------|
| *Amaranthus hybridus* (Pigweed) | 3.60 | 0.34 | 2.11 |
| *Amaranthus retroflexus* (Pigweed) | 2.30–2.85 | 0.08–0.12 | 1.10–1.14 |
| *Ambrosia artemisiifolia* (Ragweed) | 1.80–2.34 | 0.29–0.33 | 1.52–2.88 |
| *Ambrosia psilostachya* (Perennial ragweed) | 1.14–2.22 | 0.22–0.29 | 1.36–3.36 |
| *Achillea millefolium* (Yarrow) | 2.29 | 0.34 | 1.44 |
| *Acnida tamariscina* (Water hemp) | 1.52 | 0.43 | 1.75 |
| *Portulaca oleracea* (Purslane) | 4.40 | — | — |

**Confidence:** High (peer-reviewed, primary data, quantitative)

### 3.2 Harrington et al. (2006) — NZ Pasture Weeds

**Citation:** Harrington, K.C., Thatcher, A. & Kemp, P.D. "Mineral composition and nutritive value of some common pasture weeds." *New Zealand Plant Protection* 59:261-265 (2006). DOI: 10.30843/nzpp.2006.59.4414. Cited by 113+.

**Key findings (from abstract/summary):**
- Minerals such as **magnesium, manganese, copper, zinc, boron, cobalt, and selenium** were often significantly higher in weed species than in pasture grasses
- Species tested included: chicory (*Cichorium intybus*), narrow-leaved plantain (*Plantago lanceolata*), docks (*Rumex* spp.), Californian thistle (*Cirsium arvense*), and others
- Chicory and narrow-leaved plantain confirmed to provide significantly higher levels of some minerals than ryegrass/clover pasture
- Some dock species notably higher in potassium, sodium, calcium, sulfur, boron, cobalt and selenium
- **Quantitative note:** Weed species showed up to several times higher concentrations of key micronutrients compared to perennial ryegrass

**Confidence:** High (peer-reviewed, primary data, well-cited)

### 3.3 Tyler & Zarro (2021) — SARE Dynamic Accumulator Study

**Citation:** Tyler, B. & Zarro, G. "A dynamic accumulator database and field trials for six promising species." SARE/USDA Award No. 2019-38640-29877. Unadilla Community Farm, NY. Published December 2021.

**Threshold methodology:**
- USDA Dr. Duke's databases used to establish dynamic accumulator thresholds = ~200% of average nutrient concentration across all species
- 340 species qualified as dynamic accumulators
- 20 nutrients analyzed: N, P, K, Al, B, Ca, Cl, Co, Cu, Fe, I, Mg, Mn, Mo, Na, Ni, S, Se, Si, Zn

**Quantitative tissue data (2-year field trials, dried plant tissue):**

| Trial Crop | Notable Accumulations (% of average) |
|-----------|--------------------------------------|
| *Amaranthus retroflexus* (Redroot pigweed) | N 253%, P 389%, K 565%, Ca 540%, Mo 218%, Na 206% |
| *Chenopodium album* (Lambsquarters) | K 924%, Ca 461%, Mg 358%, Na 287% (in previous USDA data) |
| *Symphytum peregrinum* (Russian comfrey) | K 310%, Si 128%, Ca 153%, Mg 149% |
| *Taraxacum officinale* (Dandelion) | K 306%, Mg 447%, Ca 440%, Fe 279%, Mn 318%, Zn 438% |
| *Trifolium pratense* (Red clover) | K 243%, B 654%, Fe 266%, Mn 288% |
| *Urtica dioica* (Stinging nettle) | N 234%, Mg 283%, Ca 307%, Fe 262% |

**On-farm tissue results (actual ppm from SARE study):**

| Species | K (ppm) | Ca (ppm) | Notes |
|---------|---------|----------|-------|
| *Chenopodium album* | **40,715** | — | Exceeded DA threshold for K |
| *Symphytum peregrinum* | **52,959** | — | Exceeded DA threshold for K and Si (513 ppm) |
| *Urtica dioica* | — | **17,440** | Highest Ca of all trial crops |

**Stinging nettle liquid fertilizer (5-day steep):**
- Highest concentrations of P, B, Ca, Cu, Mn among all trial crops
- Highest nutrient carryover rates for P, B, Ca, Cu, Mn, K, Mg
- "Stinging nettle's nutrients are particularly soluble and well suited for liquid fertilizer"

**Chop-and-drop with stinging nettle:**
- Ca concentrations doubled in 0-6" and 6-12" soil horizons
- Dropped to 63% in 12-24" horizon (consistent with subsoil extraction hypothesis)

**Confidence:** High (SARE-funded, peer-reviewed methodology, 2 years, quantitative)

### 3.5 Vural (2024) — Dandelion Trace Element Study

**Citation:** Vural, A. "Trace element accumulation behavior, ability, and propensity of *Taraxacum officinale* F.H. Wigg (Dandelion)." *Environmental Science and Pollution Research* 31:16667 (2024). DOI: 10.1007/s11356-024-32293-2.

**Key findings:**
- Dandelion confirmed as a bioindicator and potential phytoremediation species
- Accumulates trace elements (TEs) in above-ground tissues
- **Cobalt concentrations:** Roots 1.32 mg/kg, stems 1.6 mg/kg, flowers 1.04 mg/kg — significantly exceeding literature values
- "T. officinale might possess a higher ability to accumulate cobalt"
- Over 400 plant species reported to accumulate metals; dandelion is one of the studied bioindicators
- Dandelion proposed for remediation due to TE accumulation in above-ground tissues

**Confidence:** High (peer-reviewed, primary data, 2024)

### 3.6 Uddin et al. (2014) — Purslane Nutrition Review

**Citation:** Uddin, M.K., Juraimi, A.S. et al. "Purslane Weed (*Portulaca oleracea*): A Prospective Plant Source of Nutrition, Omega-3 Fatty Acid, and Antioxidant Attributes." *The Scientific World Journal* (2014). DOI: 10.1155/2014/951019. PMID: 24683373.

**Quantitative data:**
- **Potassium:** 494 mg/100g fresh weight
- **Magnesium:** 68 mg/100g
- **Calcium:** 65 mg/100g
- **Ca:Mg ratio:** approximately 1:1
- Alpha-tocopherol: 22.2 mg/100g (fresh), 130 mg/100g (dry)
- Ascorbic acid: 26.6 mg/100g (fresh), 506 mg/100g (dry)
- Rich source of omega-3 fatty acids (alpha-linolenic acid)
- Snyder (1907) found purslane had 4.40% nitrogen — highest among weeds tested

**Confidence:** High (peer-reviewed review)

## Section 7: Nutrient-Weed Relationship — Soil Food Web / Succession Framework

### Elaine Ingham's Plant Succession & Weeds (via Seed World, 2024)

**Source:** Elmy, C. "What Weeds are Really Telling Us." Seed World Canada (2024).

**Key concepts:**
- Annual weeds = early successional plants (low fungal:bacterial ratio)
- Perennial weeds (thistle, dandelion) = anaerobic zone indicators, low calcium supply rate
- These perennial weeds are **"calcium and phosphate accumulators, plus they are mycorrhizal fungi-friendly"**
- Deep taproots bring up calcium, flocculating soil minerals and creating aeration
- Root exudates support microbial growth, especially in compacted areas
- Cover crops can perform the same soil remediation role as weeds
